The Five Papers

Pretraining gives a model knowledge; alignment gives it manners. This deck covers the canonical recipe for steering a base model toward human intent, the move from human to AI feedback, and the parameter-efficient methods that made all of this affordable.

01

Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback

Ouyang et al. (OpenAI), 2022 NeurIPS 2022 arXiv:2203.02155 — the InstructGPT paper, and the direct technical ancestor of ChatGPT.

The problem

A base GPT-3 trained purely on next-token likelihood is misaligned with user intent. Asked to "explain the moon landing to a six-year-old" it may continue the prompt, list more instructions, or produce something verbose and unhelpful. The pretraining objective optimises for plausible continuations of internet text — not for being helpful, truthful, or harmless when a human gives an instruction.

The contribution

A reproducible three-stage recipe that became the industry default for RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback):

  1. SFT — supervised fine-tuning on a dataset of human-written demonstrations of the desired behaviour.
  2. Reward model (RM) — collect human rankings of several model outputs per prompt, then train a model to predict that preference ordering (a scalar reward).
  3. RL with PPO — optimise the SFT policy against the reward model using Proximal Policy Optimization, with a per-token KL penalty to the SFT reference to stop the policy from drifting into reward-hacked gibberish.

The headline empirical result: outputs from the 1.3B InstructGPT model were preferred by human labellers over those from the 175B base GPT-3 — a ~100× smaller model, made better by alignment rather than scale.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

This is the mental model behind every aligned chat model you will deploy or fine-tune. The KL term is the single most important knob: too little and the policy reward-hacks; too much and it ignores the reward signal. Understanding the SFT → RM → PPO decomposition tells you exactly where a behaviour came from when you debug an assistant.

The alignment tax

RLHF can degrade raw benchmark performance on some NLP tasks — the "alignment tax". InstructGPT mitigated it by mixing pretraining gradients back into the PPO update (PPO-ptx). When you align a model, always re-measure capability benchmarks, not just preference win-rate.

02

Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback

Bai et al. (Anthropic), 2022 2022 arXiv:2212.08073

The problem

The RLHF harmlessness signal in InstructGPT depends on humans reading and labelling large volumes of toxic, harmful, or disturbing content. That is slow, expensive, psychologically taxing for labellers, and hard to scale or audit. Human preference data is also opaque: the "values" of the model live implicitly in thousands of individual labels rather than in a stated set of principles.

The contribution

RLAIF — Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback — replaces the human harm labels with an AI judge guided by a short, written "constitution" (a set of natural-language principles). Two stages:

  • Supervised (SL) stage — the model generates a response, then is prompted to critique its own answer against a constitutional principle and revise it. The revised answers form an SFT dataset (self-improvement, no human harm labels).
  • RL stage — for harmlessness preferences, an AI model picks the better of two responses according to the constitution, producing a preference dataset that trains a reward model. RLHF then proceeds as usual (often called RLAIF).

Why it matters to a practising engineer

CAI is the template for scalable oversight: using a model to supervise a model. The same critique-and-revise loop underlies modern synthetic-data pipelines and LLM-as-judge evaluation. Crucially, the model's values become editable text — you change behaviour by editing the constitution rather than re-collecting human labels.

Helpful vs harmless tension

A model trained only to be harmless learns to refuse everything ("I can't help with that"). CAI explicitly balances helpfulness (still from human feedback) against harmlessness (from AI feedback). When you write your own system prompts and refusal policies, you are authoring a miniature constitution — and inheriting the same trade-off.

03

LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models

Hu et al. (Microsoft), 2021 ICLR 2022 arXiv:2106.09685

The problem

Full fine-tuning updates every weight, so each task produces a complete copy of the model — gigabytes per task, and an optimiser state several times the model size in GPU memory. Serving dozens of fine-tuned variants, or fine-tuning at all on consumer hardware, becomes impractical.

The contribution

Freeze the pretrained weight matrix W₀ and learn the update as a low-rank decomposition. The hypothesis: the weight change needed for a downstream task has low "intrinsic rank", so it can be written as the product of two skinny matrices B (d×r) and A (r×d) with rank r ≪ d.

LoRA forward pass — only A and B are trained
# W0 frozen [d x d];  A [r x d], B [d x r];  r << d
# A ~ N(0, sigma^2),  B = 0  => deltaW starts at zero
h = x @ W0.T + (alpha / r) * (x @ A.T @ B.T)
#       frozen            trainable low-rank update

# Equivalent merged form for zero-latency inference:
W = W0 + (alpha / r) * (B @ A)

Only A and B receive gradients — reducing trainable parameters by up to ~10,000× and optimiser memory dramatically. Because B·A can be merged into W₀ after training, there is no added inference latency, unlike adapter layers.

What you store per task

Just the adapter (A, B) — often a few MB instead of multiple GB. Swap adapters at serve time to switch tasks while sharing one frozen base in memory.

Key knobs

r (rank, e.g. 8–64), alpha (scaling), and which matrices to adapt. Adapting the attention projections (q, k, v, o) is the common sweet spot.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

LoRA is the default fine-tuning method in 2024–2025. It is what makes per-customer or per-task model customisation economical, and what powers multi-adapter serving stacks (e.g. S-LoRA, vLLM adapters). If you fine-tune anything, you almost certainly fine-tune with LoRA.

The merge trick

Because W = W₀ + (alpha/r)·B·A is just another weight matrix, you can ship a merged model with identical inference cost to the base — or keep adapters separate and hot-swap them. This duality is LoRA's superpower over earlier adapter methods that added permanent runtime layers.

04

QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs

Dettmers et al., 2023 NeurIPS 2023 arXiv:2305.14314

The problem

LoRA shrinks the trainable footprint, but you still must hold the frozen base weights in GPU memory to compute gradients through them. A 65B model in 16-bit needs ~130 GB just for weights — far beyond a single consumer or workstation GPU.

The contribution

Keep the base frozen but store it in 4-bit, and back-propagate through it into 16-bit LoRA adapters. Three innovations make this lossless enough to match 16-bit fine-tuning:

  • 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) — an information-theoretically optimal data type for weights that are (approximately) normally distributed, beating plain int4/fp4.
  • Double quantisation — quantise the quantisation constants themselves, saving a further ~0.4 bits/param on average.
  • Paged optimisers — use NVIDIA unified memory to page optimiser state to CPU on memory spikes, avoiding OOM during long sequences.

The result: fine-tune a 65B-parameter model on a single 48GB GPU with negligible quality loss versus 16-bit fine-tuning. The paper's Guanaco models, trained this way, reached near-ChatGPT quality on the Vicuna benchmark.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

QLoRA democratised fine-tuning of large open-weights models. It is why a single A6000/A100-40G — or even a 24GB card for 7B–13B models — can produce a high-quality custom model. NF4 + double quant is the default config in tools like bitsandbytes, peft, and Unsloth.

Quant for training, not always for serving

QLoRA quantises the base during training. For inference you can keep the 4-bit base + adapters, or de-quantise and merge into a 16-bit model. Match the serving precision to your latency and quality targets — NF4 is optimised for the training pass, not necessarily for production throughput.

05

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Rafailov et al. (Stanford), 2023 NeurIPS 2023 arXiv:2305.18290

The problem

RLHF works, but the PPO stage is fragile and operationally heavy: you must train and host a separate reward model, then run an on-policy RL loop with reward sampling, value networks, and careful KL control. It is sensitive to hyperparameters and hard to reproduce.

The contribution

DPO shows that the RLHF objective (maximise reward under a KL constraint to a reference policy) has a closed-form optimal policy. Substituting that solution back into the Bradley–Terry preference model lets you express the reward implicitly in terms of the policy itself — collapsing the entire pipeline into a single classification-style loss on preference pairs. No reward model, no sampling, no RL loop.

DPO loss — one supervised pass over (prompt, chosen, rejected)
# pi  = policy being trained;  ref = frozen reference (the SFT model)
# y_w = preferred (chosen) response,  y_l = rejected response
log_ratio_w = logp(pi, y_w) - logp(ref, y_w)
log_ratio_l = logp(pi, y_l) - logp(ref, y_l)

# beta controls deviation from the reference (the implicit KL)
loss = -log_sigmoid(beta * (log_ratio_w - log_ratio_l))

Intuitively: push up the policy's log-probability of the chosen response and push down the rejected one, each measured relative to the frozen reference. The beta term plays the role of the KL coefficient from RLHF.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

DPO is now the default preference-tuning method for most open-weights alignment work — it runs in any standard fine-tuning loop (and composes with LoRA/QLoRA). If you have a dataset of (prompt, chosen, rejected) pairs, you can align a model in an afternoon with no RL infrastructure.

Not a total replacement

DPO needs good preference pairs and can over-fit to the reference distribution; online/on-policy variants and successors (IPO, KTO, ORPO) address its limitations. For frontier-scale harmlessness work, PPO-style RLHF and RLAIF still appear — but DPO is the pragmatic first choice for most teams.

06

What to Take Away

  • Alignment is a pipeline, not a single step. Base → SFT → preference signal → preference optimisation. InstructGPT defined the canonical SFT → RM → PPO form.
  • The feedback source is a design choice. Human feedback (RLHF) is the gold standard; AI feedback against a written constitution (Constitutional AI / RLAIF) scales oversight and removes humans from labelling toxic content.
  • The KL-to-reference term is the heart of preference tuning — explicit in PPO, implicit as beta in DPO. It keeps the model from drifting into reward-hacked nonsense.
  • LoRA made fine-tuning cheap by learning a low-rank update ΔW = B·A with no inference penalty after merging.
  • QLoRA made it cheap at scale — 4-bit NF4 base + 16-bit adapters fine-tunes a 65B model on one 48GB GPU.
  • DPO made preference tuning simple by turning RLHF into a single classification loss — no reward model, no RL loop.
